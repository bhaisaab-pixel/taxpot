The product

Positioning

The problem. A UK sole trader gets paid gross. The money lands in a current account and immediately looks like income — but a chunk of it is income tax, a chunk is Class 4 National Insurance, and if they graduated after 1998 a chunk is student loan. That reckoning arrives once a year, in January, long after the money is gone.

The user. Under-35, London-weighted, earning £3k–£40k a year on the side of a job or between contracts. They are not running a business with an accountant; they are running a Notion page and a hope. Existing calculators are annual, assume you already know your profit, and are built for people who enjoy tax.

The job. Answer one question in under two seconds: of this payment, how much is actually mine? Everything else in the app exists to make that number trustworthy.

Principles

  • One number first. “Yours to keep” is the headline; the tax breakdown is supporting evidence, not the point.
  • Local-first. Financial data stays in the browser. No accounts or central data store, which is also why there is no sync.
  • Honest, not precise. A reserve estimate that is slightly conservative is useful. A number presented as exact when it is not is a liability.
  • Reserve, don’t file. TaxPot tells you what to set aside. It does not submit anything to HMRC and should never imply that it does.

What it deliberately is not

  • Not bookkeeping. No invoices, no clients, no double-entry.
  • Not a Self Assessment filing tool. That is a regulated, HMRC-recognised integration and a different company.
  • Not an international tax tool. It covers the UK, including Scotland’s separate income tax bands; other countries need different rules.

Tax engine

Specification

The engine is the product. Everything else is presentation, and a wrong number here is worse than no product at all.

The marginal model

A payment cannot be taxed in isolation, because UK income tax is progressive. The same £1,000 is worth £1,000 to someone who has earned nothing this year and £510 to someone already in the higher-rate band. So TaxPot never applies a rate to a payment. It computes a full-year liability twice and takes the difference:

split(payment) = liability(ytd + payment) − liability(ytd)

where ytd is the sum of every saved payment this tax year, and liability returns income tax, Class 4 NI and student loan separately. This is the single most important design decision in the app and the thing v0.1 got wrong.

Order of operations

  • Total income = other employment income + self-employed profit. Both feed the income tax and student loan calculations; only the profit feeds Class 4 NI.
  • Pension relief reduces taxable income by profit × rate. It does not reduce Class 4 NI — a common and expensive misunderstanding.
  • Personal allowance is tapered by £1 for every £2 of income above £100,000, reaching zero at £125,140.
  • Income tax attributable to the side income = tax(total) − tax(other income alone). This puts the profit on top of the salary, which is where HMRC puts it.
  • Student loan is charged on income above the plan threshold, again computed as a difference so a salary that already crosses the threshold is not double-counted.

Staying current

gov.uk

Every rate in this app expires. Thresholds change each 6 April, and a tax tool that is quietly a year out of date is the worst kind of broken — it keeps working, and keeps being wrong. Two things now guard against that.

The reminder

rateFreshness() compares today against the tax year in force and against when the table was last verified. It reports one of four states, and the app surfaces the bad ones on the main screen rather than burying them:

  • Missing — today falls in a tax year the app has no rates for. Shown as a red alert; nothing in the app should be trusted until it is fixed.
  • Stale — rates were last verified before the current year began on 6 April.
  • Due — never checked from this browser, or not for six months.
  • OK — verified within the current tax year and checked recently.

The refresh

Check rates against gov.uk in the Method tab reads the published figures straight from the source and compares them, line by line, with the table the app is using. It works because gov.uk’s Content API returns JSON and sends Access-Control-Allow-Origin: *, so a static page with no server can read it directly.

SourceWhat is read
Income tax rates and bandsPersonal allowance, the £100k taper, and the three England/Wales/NI bands
Self-employed National InsuranceClass 4 limits and rates, Class 2 small profits threshold and weekly rate
Repaying your student loanAll five plan thresholds
Scottish income taxAll six Scottish bands, for the current and previous year

Why it never updates silently

Reading tax rates out of a government web page with regular expressions is useful, and it is not something to trust blindly — a page redesign could turn a half-match into a plausible wrong number. So the refresh never applies anything on its own. It shows every figure side by side, marked matches, differs or not read, and only changes a rate after you have seen the difference and confirmed it. Applied rates are stored separately from the built-in table and can be restored with one button.

The extractors are pure functions tested against saved copies of the real gov.uk text in tests/fixtures/. That matters more than it sounds: a scraper without tests is a liability, and these tests are what will tell you the page layout changed rather than letting a wrong figure through.

Running the check today reports every one of the 43 figures matching, across both regions — which is gov.uk independently confirming the whole rate table rather than anyone taking it on trust.

Planning ahead: 2027/28 to 2029/30

The app carries the next three tax years so you can plan against them. What makes that defensible rather than reckless is that most of the important figures are not guesses at all.

Budget 2025 froze the personal allowance at £12,570, the basic rate limit at £37,700 and the Class 4 lower and upper profits limits through 2030/31. For the next three years those are set in statute. What genuinely is not yet published is everything that gets uprated each April: student loan thresholds, the Class 2 amounts, and the Scottish bands, which the Scottish Parliament sets annually.

So every figure in a future year declares its own basis, and the app shows it:

BasisApplies toWhat it means
legislatedPersonal allowance, income tax bands, Class 4 thresholdsFixed in statute for that year. As reliable as this year’s figures.
assumedClass 4 rates, trading allowance, VAT thresholdNo change announced, but not legislated that far ahead.
estimatedStudent loan thresholds, Class 2, Scottish bandsUprated every April and not yet published. Carried forward from 2026/27 as a placeholder.

A provisional year is labelled in the year picker, warned about on the main screen, and broken down component by component in the Method tab. The numbers still calculate properly — it is a plan you can act on, not a stub.

The trap this nearly walked into

Adding future years created a hazard that only showed up in testing. gov.uk publishes the figures for whichever year is currently in force. Sitting on 2028/29 and pressing refresh would have read today’s 2026/27 student loan thresholds, written them into 2028/29, stamped the year verified and cleared the provisional flag — converting an honest estimate into a confident lie.

So the refresh now reads which year gov.uk is talking about and refuses to apply it to any other one, explaining why. The comparison is still shown, because it usefully confirms that the frozen figures are right; it just is not allowed to masquerade as confirmation of the parts that are still estimates.

Accessibility & devices

Audited

The target user is under-35 and London-weighted, but the app runs wherever someone opens it — and a UK freelancer’s phone is not a fixed thing. This section is the audit against real device constraints, not just a desktop-Chrome check, done because it is easy to make something that only ever gets tested on the machine that built it.

Fixed in this pass

  • Keyboard tabs. The five section tabs now use roving tabindex with Left/Right/Home/End navigation, per the WAI-ARIA APG pattern — previously every tab sat in the Tab order individually, which is usable but not what a screen-reader user expects from something announced as a tab list.
  • A modal that actually was one. The settings drawer declared aria-modal="true", which tells a screen reader the rest of the page is inert — but nothing stopped a sighted keyboard user from Tabbing straight out of it into that supposedly-inert background. Tab/Shift+Tab now trap inside the drawer, and closing it returns focus to whatever button opened it, rather than dropping focus back at the top of the document.
  • Named, not generic, labels. The CSV-import table’s per-row checkbox and “treat as” select had no accessible name beyond the visible column header. Each now carries the actual transaction — “Import Coffee at Pret, £4.50 on 3 Jun 26” — so a screen-reader user tabbing through rows knows which row they are on without cross-referencing a table they cannot see.
  • Touch targets. The settings-gear button (34px) and each ledger row’s delete button (a 15px icon in 5px of padding — about 25px) were both under the ~40–44px that Android’s and iOS’s own design guidelines call comfortable, and tighter than that matters more on the less precise capacitive digitizers common in sub-£120 phones than it does on a flagship. Both are now 40–44px; the import table’s checkboxes sit in a 40×40 tap area rather than a bare native checkbox rendered at native size.
  • Decimal keypads. A bare type="number" input shows whatever numeric keyboard the OS and keyboard app decide on, and on several budget Android keyboards that keyboard has no decimal point key at all — a genuine, commonly-reported mobile-web bug, not a theoretical one. Every money field now sets inputmode="decimal" explicitly (the pension-percentage field gets inputmode="numeric" instead, since it is never fractional).

Checked and already sound

Worth recording what did not need fixing, so it does not get re-litigated later:

  • Colour contrast. Every semantic colour (tax/loan/pension/danger/warning) was measured against its actual background with the real WCAG relative-luminance formula, in both themes — the tightest result is 4.62:1 (amber tax figures on the light background), everything else clears 5:1. All pass AA for small text.
  • Zoom and text scaling. The viewport meta tag sets no maximum-scale and does not disable user-scalable, so pinch-zoom works. Every font size in the app is set in rem, not fixed pixels, so a phone’s system-level “larger text” setting — commonly turned up on smaller or older screens — scales the whole app correctly rather than being silently ignored.
  • Narrow screens. Tested at 360×800 (by a wide margin the most common Android CSS viewport in India) and at 320×568 (older and Android Go-class devices still in active use): no horizontal page scroll at either width, no overlapping header elements, no clipped text. The one thing that does scroll sideways — the CSV-import preview table — scrolls inside its own container, deliberately, rather than dragging the whole page with it.
  • Fonts on slow connections. The three Google Fonts are loaded with &display=swap and both font origins are preconnected, so text renders immediately in a system fallback and swaps in rather than staying invisible while a webfont downloads over a throttled connection.
  • Storage failures. Private browsing and some locked-down MDM/corporate browsers throw on localStorage access. Every read and write is already guarded, and the app tells the user outright — “this browser is blocking local storage” — rather than silently failing to save.

Known gaps, and why they are staying gaps

Two device categories are real in the Indian market and not meaningfully supported here — named rather than quietly ignored:

Device classWhat breaksWhy it is not being fixed
Opera Mini, and Chrome/Android’s own aggressive “Lite mode”-style data saversOpera Mini’s default (“Extreme”) mode renders pages on Opera’s own servers and ships back a static image or stripped-down page; it does not reliably execute the JavaScript this entire app depends on. The app would render as an empty shell or nothing at all.Supporting it would mean a server-rendered fallback path — which means a server, which is the one thing the “your data never leaves your browser” promise depends on not existing. This is a direct trade-off against the core privacy claim, not an oversight.
KaiOS feature phones (JioPhone and similar)These are non-touch, D-pad-and-numeric-keypad devices. The keyboard-navigation work in this pass (roving tabindex, focus trapping) happens to help a D-pad user get through the tab bar and the settings drawer, but the rest of the interface — a split calculator built around tapping amounts and buttons — was never designed for select-and-click navigation as the primary input.Real support would mean redesigning the primary interaction model for a form-based, single-column, D-pad-first flow — a different product, not an accessibility pass on this one. Neither gap is silent: both are a real, current limitation of a client-only, zero-backend design, not something a future patch quietly forgot. If either ever matters enough to fix, it is a scope decision — not a bug report.

Architecture

Reference

One HTML file, no build step, no dependencies, no server. Open it and it runs; put it on any static host and it ships. That constraint is deliberate — it is what makes “your data never leaves your browser” a fact rather than a promise.

v0.1 pulled Tailwind, Alpine and Lucide from three CDNs. The later build removed all three and uses hand-written CSS and vanilla JavaScript. The app logic works without those CDN dependencies.

Files

  • index.html — the application. One file, no build step, no dependencies.
  • tests/engine.test.js — 289 assertions. Extracts the engine from index.html between the ENGINE:START / ENGINE:END sentinels and runs it in Node, so the single-file architecture survives without giving up tests.
  • tools/sync-engine.js — copies that same engine block into this page’s demo, so the documentation can never show different arithmetic from the product.
  • tests/fixtures/ — saved copies of the real gov.uk text the rate extractors are tested against.
  • tools/smoke-extractors.js — prints what the extractors read out of those fixtures, and diffs it against the built-in table.
  • product-docs.html — this document.
  • brand/ — the Canva-designed share card reference, launch graphic and social preview, plus the written palette, type and mark spec.
  • icons/ and manifest.webmanifest — the app icon set and the manifest that makes TaxPot installable to a phone homescreen.
  • tools/make-icons.py — redraws the app icons from the same mark the app uses, so the homescreen icon cannot drift.
  • archive/index.v0.1.html — the original handover build, kept for reference.
  • README.md — launch copy and how to run.

Why the engine has sentinels

Shipping one self-contained file and having a real test suite normally pull in opposite directions. Marking the pure-function core with comment sentinels resolves it: the browser sees one inline script, while Node extracts and tests exactly the code that ships. Five of the defects listed above were found this way and would not have been visible from the interface — including one that would have left the highest earners untaxed.

Known limits of the local-first choice

  • Clearing site data removes the local ledger. A previous JSON backup can restore it; without a backup, the data is lost.
  • No cross-device use. A phone and a laptop are two separate ledgers.
  • No analytics on user data, which means product decisions have to come from conversations rather than dashboards.

Roadmap & money

Plan

Where it stands

  • v1.0 — shipped. Correct engine, expenses, Self Assessment forecast, CSV import, Scottish bands, backup and restore, and verified rate tables.
  • v1.1 — shipped. The freshness reminder and the gov.uk rate refresh. Rates can no longer go quietly stale.
  • v1.2 — shipped. The next three tax years, each figure declaring whether it is legislated, assumed or estimated.
  • v1.3–v1.8 — shipped. Brand assets, phone installation, a rolling 12-month VAT check, expense categories, accessibility fixes, and the build tracker.
  • Next: get it in front of ten people. Test whether the reserve figure and accountant export answer real needs before building payment gating or sync.

The thing to watch

Rates going stale remains a risk, so the app shows a freshness reminder and lets the user compare published figures against gov.uk — see Staying current. It carries 2027/28 to 2029/30 as provisional years, with each figure marked legislated, assumed or estimated. A person must verify and update those years as official rates are published. Years beyond 2029/30 are not generated automatically.

On the visual design

The share card and the launch graphic were laid out in Canva and exported to brand/. The share card is kept as a reference rather than shipped as an image, because the numbers on it have to be the viewer’s own — so the layout was rebuilt in canvas and now renders at 1600×900 with the same colour-coded pots and hairline rules.

Worth recording: of the eight designs the generator produced, two invented figures — one rendered a £90 student loan as “£900.00”. On a financial asset that is not a styling problem, and it is the reason every number on these cards was checked against the app’s real output before use.

The app is now installable to a phone homescreen and produces a proper link preview when shared — two things whose absence made it feel like a demo rather than a product. The mark itself is defined once and redrawn from that single definition everywhere it appears, which is what made two rounds of correction cheap: blowing it up to icon size revealed a fill line sitting low enough to read as a smile, and testing it down at favicon size revealed a neck so tall it read as a lab flask and collapsed into a blob below 32px. Both were fixed in one place and propagated to all four surfaces.

The second of those came out of a Canva generation round that produced nothing usable — gradient backgrounds where flat was specified, one saucepan, and no correct fill line — but whose rounder silhouettes were a fair criticism of the mark in use. The generated art was discarded; the critique was kept. That is usually the honest yield from a generative design pass.

Monetisation

The README proposes a £10/month Stripe payment link with manual onboarding for the first ten users. That is the right shape for validation and the wrong shape to build on: a client-side userIsPro flag is bypassable in one line of console, so the paywall only works while nobody cares enough to look. Acceptable for the first ten users, replaced before the hundredth. Nothing is wired up yet — it needs a real Stripe link, and gating a local-first app with no server is a genuine design problem rather than a missing afternoon.

The harder question is what £10/month buys. Splitting a payment is a two-second interaction that a spreadsheet also does. The willingness to pay sits in the things that require work — CSV import, the payment-on-account forecast, an export an accountant will accept — and those now exist. Price the annual anxiety, not the calculator.

Free forever: the split, the pots, the year-to-date view. Paid: import, forecast, export, multi-year. That line keeps the viral loop intact — the free tier is the thing people screenshot.

Open questions

  • Does the user want a conservative buffer (round the reserve up) or the exact figure? Conservative builds trust; exact builds confidence. Worth testing with the first ten.
  • Sole traders only, or limited companies too? The tax model is entirely different and would double the engine.
  • Do users understand when the built-in £1,000 trading allowance beats their actual expenses?
